Project Overview

I designed the Car Crash PSA Project to connect physical science with decisions students encounter as drivers and passengers. The project began with a structured investigation of vehicle speed, collision forces, and passenger safety, followed by a public service announcement (PSA) intended for students at our school.

The investigation asked students to record and interpret modeled crash data, use equations, and explain safety features through Newton’s laws. The PSA was intended to give that work an audience by asking students to turn scientific evidence into a clear safety message.

Learning Objectives

I asked students to investigate how changing speed or collision type affects the values reported by a crash model. They were expected to compare results, show calculations, and explain the relationship between a rapid change in velocity, acceleration, and net force.

Students also explored inertia, interaction forces, and how seat belts, airbags, and crumple zones help protect passengers. The worksheet asked them to evaluate the visualizer’s publisher, purpose, and limitations before deciding which evidence they could responsibly use.

For the PSA, my goal was for students to connect physics concepts with investigation findings and outside research, cite sources, and propose a specific action for a teenage audience. These were the intended learning goals for the creative assignment.

Instructional Design

I organized the investigation around predictions, controlled comparisons, calculations, and reflection. Students first considered their assumptions about crash danger. They then held collision type constant while changing speed, followed by comparisons of collision types at the same speed. This structure gave them a way to examine variables systematically.

The packet guided students from recorded observations to explanations. It supplied equations and conversions for force calculations and asked students to apply Newton’s laws to passenger motion and vehicle safety features. Its source-evaluation questions also required students to consider the limits of a simplified model.

I provided a brainstorming sheet to help students identify an audience message, choose a format, and collect evidence and sources. The rubrics described expectations for scientific understanding, research, persuasion, presentation, and individual reflection, with different deliverables for smaller and larger groups. Those criteria still needed a clearer set of directions to guide the production process.

Implementation

I began with the Crash by the Numbers investigation packet. It directed students to use the interactive Crash Injury Visualizer to record deceleration, equivalent fall height, braking distance, and modeled injury risk. Students were asked to compare the displayed values and explain what they suggested about speed and collision type.

Later questions moved into calculations and scientific reasoning. Students converted deceleration into acceleration, applied F = ma to a hypothetical passenger, and considered why increasing stopping time can reduce average force. The packet concluded with safety features, source evaluation, and a reflection on a message their peers needed to hear.

The planned next step was to carry that evidence into a PSA using the brainstorming sheet and group-size rubrics. This transition was the weaker part of implementation. The directions did not give students enough clarity or structure to complete the creative assignment successfully.

Reflection & Future Improvements

The project produced mixed results. The investigation packet was successful in helping students explore the relationship between vehicle speed, collision forces, and passenger safety. Students appeared interested in the subject, particularly because it connected with their experiences driving and riding in vehicles.

The PSA component did not go as planned. I wanted students to apply their scientific understanding to a creative public safety message, but my directions lacked the clarity and structure they needed to complete the assignment successfully.

In a future implementation, I would redesign the PSA instructions with clearer expectations, more specific requirements, and examples of successful final products. I would connect those examples to the rubric so students could see how scientific explanations, evidence, and a call to action fit together in a finished message.

I would also add checkpoints for a proposed message, an evidence outline, and a draft before students move into production. These would give me opportunities to address misunderstandings and clarify requirements earlier. This experience reinforced how much instructional scaffolding matters when students move from a structured investigation into independent, creative work.
