Master’s action research · University of Cincinnati

How Artificial Intelligence in Secondary Classrooms Shapes Student Thinking and Mastery of STEM Content

Research Overview

For my master’s action research, I designed and studied a four-week unit on atomic structure in my tenth-grade physical science classes. I built AI into lessons I already taught and gave it a specific role. Students used its feedback to check their reasoning, clarify concepts, and revise their explanations.

Research Objectives

I wanted to understand how structured AI use might support students’ understanding of atomic structure and how it might shape their confidence, engagement, and perceptions of learning science. I was also interested in whether students could use feedback while retaining responsibility for their own reasoning.

Methodology

I designed the assessments and surveys, then collected evidence across three sections of the same course. I summarized performance on selected assessment questions and used matched survey responses where available. I reviewed open-ended reflections and grouped recurring themes, including when students found AI useful and what made it frustrating.

The study took place over one unit with one teacher and no control group. Survey participation also varied. I interpret the results as classroom evidence about this implementation. The design cannot isolate AI’s contribution or establish whether the changes would persist.

Classroom Implementation

I embedded prompts for MagicSchool AI in existing worksheets and activities. Students could ask follow-up questions, check their work, or request another explanation. In one activity, I asked students to compare AI-generated definitions with definitions I provided so they could practice evaluating the output against a classroom source.

I used assignment prompts to guide students’ interactions and remained available for help. Formal instruction in writing prompts was outside the scope of this unit. Students had opportunities to work through questions independently and could turn to me when they needed clarification.

Lessons Learned

I observed stronger performance on the targeted atomic structure questions and greater self-reported confidence. Overall engagement changed little. In their reflections, students often described using AI to clarify concepts or check their work. Some received answers that were too broad or missed their question. Others found teacher explanations more helpful.

Those responses showed me where the design needed more support. A prompt built into a worksheet can give students a starting point, but students also need practice judging whether an answer addresses their question and deciding how to use it in their own work.

Reflection & Future Implications

In a future unit, I would explicitly teach AI literacy. I would model how to ask a focused question, check an answer against trusted course materials, and revise an explanation in one’s own words. I would also make clear when working independently or asking a teacher for guidance better serves the learning goal.

I would study the approach over a longer period to see whether students become more independent in how they use feedback. This project shaped how I approach technology in instructional design. I start with the learning goal, define where feedback can help, and use learner evidence to decide what to revise.
